> **Status:** Draft for Directorate approval. On approval this policy replaces the Anaphylaxis and Adrenaline Auto-Injector (EpiPen) Policy (Nov 2024), which will be retired from the vault.

## 1. Purpose

Nudge Education works with young people in their homes, in community venues and online. Some of them live with allergies that can cause anaphylaxis, a severe and potentially life-threatening reaction. This policy sets out how Nudge Education keeps those young people safe: how allergy needs are identified before work begins, how practitioners are trained and equipped to prevent and respond to a reaction, and how incidents and near misses are recorded and learnt from.

It is written to meet the expectations of the Department for Education's statutory guidance *Allergy safety in schools* (July 2026, issued under section 100 of the Children and Families Act 2014 as amended by section 34 of the Children's Wellbeing and Schools Act 2026). That guidance places duties on maintained schools, academies and pupil referral units. Nudge Education is a non-school alternative provision and intervention provider and is not a duty-holder under it; the guidance states that out-of-scope settings should treat it as good practice, and Nudge Education does so. Commissioning schools and local authorities retain their own statutory responsibilities for the children they place with us, and this policy is designed to let them discharge those responsibilities with confidence.

## 2. Scope

This policy applies to all Nudge Education staff and practitioners, in every setting in which Nudge Education delivers: the young person's home, community and public venues, travel between them, and online delivery. It applies to Nudge Education Online (NEO) as set out in the NEO Online Addendum below.

It covers all allergies capable of causing a serious reaction, whether to food, insect stings, medication, latex or other triggers, and whether or not the young person has been prescribed an adrenaline auto-injector.

The mechanics of Individual Healthcare Plans, medication handling and record-keeping are set out in the [[supporting-young-people-with-medical-conditions|Supporting Young People with Medical Conditions Policy]] and the [[administration-of-medication-policy|Administration of Medication Policy]]. This policy does not repeat them; it sets the allergy-specific expectations that sit on top of them.

## 3. Definitions

**Allergy**: an immune response to a substance (allergen) that is harmless to most people. Reactions range from mild (itching, rash, swelling of the lips) to severe.

**Anaphylaxis**: a severe, rapid-onset allergic reaction that can affect breathing, circulation or consciousness. It is a medical emergency. Signs include difficulty breathing or swallowing, wheeze or persistent cough, swelling of the tongue or throat, a sudden drop in alertness, pale or floppy presentation, and a feeling of impending doom. Skin symptoms are common but may be absent.

**Adrenaline auto-injector (AAI)**: a prescribed single-use device for injecting adrenaline into the outer thigh to treat anaphylaxis. Common brands include EpiPen, Jext and Emerade. The term AAI is used throughout this policy; it covers all brands.

**Individual Healthcare Plan (IHP)**: the written plan for a young person with a medical condition, as defined in the Supporting Young People with Medical Conditions Policy. For a young person with a serious allergy the IHP is also their allergy action plan.

**Near miss**: any event in which a young person with a known allergy was, or could have been, exposed to their allergen during Nudge Education activity, whether or not a reaction followed.

## 4. Principles

Allergy safety is a safeguarding matter and the welfare of the young person comes first. No young person should be excluded from any part of their intervention because of an allergy; the intervention is adapted around the allergy. Practitioners act on the information they hold and never assume a reaction is unlikely. Where there is doubt whether a reaction is anaphylaxis, it is treated as anaphylaxis: adrenaline is given and 999 is called.

## 5. Roles and responsibilities

**Director of Operations (Allergy Safety Lead)** holds senior responsibility for this policy: keeps it current against statutory guidance and clinical best practice, ensures training provision is in place and completed, reviews incident and near-miss records at least termly, and reports significant incidents to the Directorate.

**Regional Leads and Assistant Regional Leads** ensure that allergy information is sought at referral and Initial Assessment, that an IHP is in place before the first session where one is needed, that the intervention risk assessment reflects the allergy, and that the allocated practitioner has read both before delivery begins.

**Education Intervention Co-ordinators** check at planning and review points that the IHP and risk assessment remain accurate, and that any change in the young person's allergy status reported by the family or commissioner is actioned.

**Practitioners and all delivery staff** read and follow the IHP and risk assessment; know where the young person's AAIs are during every session; keep sessions free of the young person's allergens as far as reasonably possible; recognise the signs of a reaction and respond as this policy requires; and record and report incidents and near misses on the same day.

**Parents and carers** tell Nudge Education about any allergy before work begins and about any change in diagnosis, triggers or treatment; supply two in-date, prescribed AAIs for every session where one has been prescribed; and replace used or expired devices.

**Commissioners** share the young person's existing allergy information, IHP or school allergy action plan at referral, and tell Nudge Education of any change during the placement.

## 6. Identifying allergy needs before delivery

Allergy information is requested from the commissioner and the family at referral and confirmed at Initial Assessment. Where a young person has a diagnosed allergy capable of causing a serious reaction, the Assistant Regional Lead ensures that an IHP is in place before the first session, following the Supporting Young People with Medical Conditions Policy, and that the intervention risk assessment records the allergen, the settings and activities in which exposure is plausible, and the controls agreed.

Where the family or commissioner cannot supply an IHP, Nudge Education prepares one with the family and seeks clinical input through the commissioner or the young person's GP. Delivery does not begin for a young person with a prescribed AAI until the IHP, the risk assessment and the AAIs are in place.

## 7. Preventing exposure during sessions

Practitioners plan each session around the young person's known allergens. In practice this means: no food or drink is brought into a session, offered or shared without first checking the IHP, and never where it contains or may contain the young person's allergen; community venues, activities and travel are risk-assessed for exposure, including food outlets, cooking activities and environments where insect stings are likely; materials used in sessions (art materials, gloves, equipment) are checked for latex or other recorded triggers; and where the young person's allergy is airborne or contact-based, the practitioner avoids consuming or carrying the allergen on the day of a session.

The young person is involved in decisions about their own allergy safety in a way appropriate to their age and understanding, and is never made to feel singled out by the precautions taken.

## 8. Adrenaline auto-injectors

Every young person who has been prescribed an AAI must have two in-date devices with them for every session, supplied by the family. The practitioner confirms at the start of each session that the devices are present, in date and where the IHP says they are. Devices stay with or close to the young person; they are never locked away or left in a vehicle.

Where the devices are missing, expired or inaccessible at the start of a session, the practitioner does not proceed with activity that carries exposure risk, contacts the family and the Education Intervention Co-ordinator, and records the event as a near miss.

Nudge Education does not hold a stock of spare AAIs. The regulations that permit schools to buy and hold spare devices without prescription apply to schools only, and Nudge Education's delivery model, with practitioners working across many homes and venues, does not allow a safe or lawful central stock. If this position changes in law, this policy will be updated.

Expired or used devices are returned to the family for safe disposal; practitioners do not dispose of them.

## 9. Responding to an allergic reaction

For a mild reaction (skin symptoms only, no breathing or circulation signs), the practitioner follows the IHP, which will usually specify an antihistamine supplied by the family, stays with the young person, watches for progression and informs the family.

For suspected anaphylaxis, the practitioner acts immediately and in this order:

1. Give the AAI into the outer thigh, through clothing if necessary, as trained. Do not wait to see whether symptoms worsen.
2. Call 999 and say "anaphylaxis". Give the location precisely; in a community venue, send someone to meet the ambulance.
3. Lie the young person flat with legs raised. If breathing is difficult, let them sit up with legs outstretched. Do not let them stand or walk, even if they feel better.
4. If there is no improvement after five minutes, give the second AAI.
5. Stay with the young person until the ambulance arrives and hand over what was given and when. Give the used device(s) to the paramedics.
6. Contact the family and the Education Intervention Co-ordinator as soon as the young person is in the care of emergency services.

A young person who has received adrenaline always goes to hospital, even if they appear to have recovered.

## 10. Training

Every practitioner and member of delivery staff completes allergy awareness training before working with young people and renews it at least annually. The training covers recognising the signs of an allergic reaction and of anaphylaxis, the use of each type of AAI in current use, the emergency procedure in section 9, and the prevention expectations in section 7. First aid training on its own is not sufficient and is not accepted in place of allergy awareness training.

Where a practitioner is allocated to a young person with a prescribed AAI, the Assistant Regional Lead confirms before the first session that the practitioner's training is current and that they have practised with a trainer device of the brand the young person uses. Training completion is recorded on the central training record and is reviewed by the Allergy Safety Lead.

## 11. Recording, reporting and learning

Any administration of an AAI, any allergic reaction and any near miss is recorded on the same day on the incident form in the approved system, in line with the Administration of Medication Policy and the Child Protection and Safeguarding Policy's recording standards. The family and the commissioner are informed the same day. The Allergy Safety Lead reviews every anaphylaxis incident and every near miss, identifies what should change in the IHP, the risk assessment, the practitioner's practice or this policy, and ensures the change is made.

Concerns about the management of a young person's allergy at home, repeated unexplained exposure, or inconsistency between reported and observed symptoms are safeguarding matters and are reported to the Regional Designated Safeguarding Lead under the Child Protection and Safeguarding Policy.

## 12. Information and data protection

Allergy information is health data. It is held on approved Nudge Education systems only, shared with the practitioners who need it to keep the young person safe, and handled under the [[../01-all-company/data-protection-and-information-security|Data Protection and Information Security Policy]].

## 13. Monitoring and review

The Allergy Safety Lead monitors IHP completion for young people with recorded allergies, training completion and currency, and the incident and near-miss log, and reports to the Directorate at least termly. This policy is reviewed at least annually, and sooner following any anaphylaxis incident, any change to the statutory guidance or the regulations on spare adrenaline devices, or any change in the law that brings non-school alternative provision into scope.

## NEO Online Addendum

> **Status:** Draft, for approval by the Director, NEO & Head of School alongside the canonical policy.

NEO is fully online and NEO staff are never physically present with a learner, so the prevention, AAI and emergency-response sections of this policy do not apply to NEO delivery. What NEO does is narrower and is already set out in the NEO addendum to the Supporting Young People with Medical Conditions Policy: hold the learner's allergy information supplied by the family at admission and on change; recognise during a live session the visible and audible signs that a reaction may be in progress (sudden breathing difficulty, swelling, distress, loss of alertness); and run the in-session medical event procedure in that addendum, staying on the call, contacting the named emergency contact and, where needed, asking for emergency services to be called.

The one allergy-specific addition for NEO is practical: where a learner has a serious allergy, the family's admission information records whether an appropriate adult is in the home during scheduled sessions and where the learner's AAIs are kept, so that NEO staff can prompt the adult to use them if a reaction is suspected. NEO staff do not instruct the learner or the adult on how to administer adrenaline beyond relaying the words of the learner's own IHP.

NEO staff complete the same annual allergy awareness training as Nudge Education practitioners, so that they can recognise a reaction on screen.
